A fictional place-based ecosystem

Morrow Estuary / Everyday Futures

A coastal valley with an old working harbour, protected wetlands and scattered inland settlements. Visitors support businesses, while residents face uneven transport, care responsibilities and limited year-round opportunities.

The fictional Everyday Futures partnership is exploring a Care & Access Commons: a small programme linking community transport, everyday services and stewardship. The question is how people can continue to live and work here without treating ecological protection or local livelihoods as optional.

The shared aspiration is liveability. The disagreement is over who benefits, whose work counts and which commitments the partnership can actually sustain.

This is not Urdaibai under another name. There is no museum proposal, no copied stakeholder profile and no transfer of the report’s findings into this fictional community. The source guides the kinds of tensions worth examining.

What the client can do with this

Support the conditions that make action possible.

The output is an evidence-linked set of choices and experiments—not a recommendation to make everybody collaborate more.

A / Listening with consequence

Make access and follow-through part of the process

Adapt conversations to care and work schedules. Record each concern’s destination in a brief or decision, including why it was not taken forward.

Episode contrast: E01 records a concern; E02 carries new concerns into a revised brief.

Test: follow the next three decision cycles. Compare whose concerns enter, what changes and what participants recognise in the response.

A changed brief is not equivalent to representation, consent or improved services.

B / Agreement without forced consensus

Define a bounded trial with explicit exclusions

State who it serves, what it will not do, ecological boundaries, decision authority and the review date. Keep deferred concerns visible.

Episode contrast: E05 has shared aspirations but no plan; E06 authorises a limited trial.

Test: check whether participants understand the limits and whether the trial delivers its stated function without concealing exclusion.

Agreement on a trial is not agreement on the wider development model.

C / Continuity with responsibility

Fund the operating and review work

Agree paid time, an accountable service owner and resident access review before a pilot ends.

Episode contrast: E11 retains contacts but loses service; E12 creates a time-limited operating commitment.

Test: track actual journeys, operating costs, unmet need and who is missing from service records over a further quarter.

Continuity is only valuable if the continuing function merits support.

An intervention hypothesis → a bounded test → new episodes and independent checks → a revised decision.

Before/after comparison can support learning, but does not by itself establish causality. Demand, budgets, staff, weather and other initiatives can change the result.

How the method would work

Listen for meaning. Reconstruct action. Compare both.

ListenEveryday concerns, meanings and competing priorities.
ReconstructConcrete demands, sequences, participants, resources and outcomes.
InterpretCompare episodes with participants; retain disagreements and gaps.
TestChange a condition and observe who gains access and what happens.

Interview guide / 45–60 minutes

  1. What is becoming harder or more possible in this place? What matters to you?
  2. Describe a recent occasion when people needed to act together on that concern.
  3. What triggered it? What happened first, then next?
  4. Who got involved, and who could not? What did their involvement require?
  5. Which contacts formed or became useful? Which approaches failed?
  6. Who could decide, authorise resources or stop the action? What happened to disagreement?
  7. What changed, for whom? Who might describe the result differently?
  8. What remained after the event or funding ended? Describe a contrasting occasion.

A realistic first pilot

One delimited partnership; three priority functions; 10–15 interviews across institutional and less-organised voices. Aim to reconstruct 12–20 unique episodes, without claiming saturation in advance.

Go beyond referrals from established organisations. Use outreach around shifts, care duties, language and geography. Record who declines or cannot participate without identifying them publicly.

Triangulate a selection with another participant and authorised documents. Hold a collective interpretation session, offer separate channels for people unable to attend and record differences rather than manufacturing agreement.

Deliverables: episode records, an interactive repertoire explorer, a comparative reading, a decision workshop and a 90-day test plan.

What AI contributes—and what needs human judgement

An LLM can propose codes, retrieve supporting passages and compare candidate patterns. It must not fill gaps or invent relationships. Mark information as explicit, inferred, disputed or unknown; retain excerpt references and coding revisions. Review a shared selection with two analysts before applying a coding approach more broadly.

Narrative concerns are not permanent actor types. The same person may express different priorities across situations. Separately code demand, intended function, reported coordination, enabling/blocking conditions, reported outcome and persistence. Several accounts of one event remain one episode.

What these visuals mean

All eight people, organisations, excerpts and results are invented. The links are curated illustrations of contacts and coordination narrated in these excerpts. Their shape, lengths, positions and shaded regions are not measured properties. A faded actor is not mentioned as involved in the selected episode; fading does not prove absence.

This demonstration does not estimate trust, centrality, adaptive capacity, population preferences or intervention effects. It does not test automated extraction accuracy. All episodes have one narrator and no triangulation. Synthetic examples can explain a product; they cannot validate the theory or its commercial demand.

Responsible delivery in a real community

Agree consent, access to excerpts and the level of public disclosure. Use pseudonyms or aggregated roles as appropriate. Offer participants ways to contest interpretations without exposure to employers or officials. Avoid publishing personal relationship diagrams by default. The purpose is to inform deliberation and accountable decisions, not to classify residents or predict their compliance.

Five-minute client conversation

You have a partnership.
What can it actually bring into action?

0:00–0:45 / The problem

A place can have many initiatives and still leave residents unable to reach care, work or decisions. Listing actors and recording opinions does not explain how collective action becomes possible.

0:45–1:30 / The proposition

We combine listening with concrete episodes of action. We compare how the same partnership organises itself when it needs to listen, mobilise, negotiate, test a service, respond to disruption or keep going.

1:30–3:00 / The product

Show “Negotiate”, then switch E05 to E06. Common aspirations fail to produce a plan; a bounded agreement enables a trial without resolving every disagreement. Open the episode to show the interpretation’s source and limits.

3:00–4:15 / The decision

Show “Sustain action”. A pilot can lose operating capacity while relationships remain. The question becomes what work, authority and accountability need support—and whether the service merits continuation.

4:15–5:00 / The invitation

Commission a bounded paid pilot: three functions, diverse interviews, reconstructed episodes and a collective interpretation workshop. Use the result to choose one verifiable change and observe it for 90 days.

Understand the conditions that turn relationships into action—and who benefits when they do.

This is a demonstration of a possible service, not evidence from a real client. Scope, duration and price depend on access, inclusion needs and the depth of verification.
